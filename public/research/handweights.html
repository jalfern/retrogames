The network has 144,547 weights; v4 uses 14% of them. The autopilot is planner.js, a hand-written algorithm that predicts the landing point and picks which brick to hit. It clears all 7 rounds in 61,472 ticks.

The aim edit helped most runs (best run 16,881) but caused a new failure: in 2 of 20 runs the ball locked into a repeating loop on round 1 and never finished it. That is the next edit to try.

How it was built

  1. v1, chaser. Layer 1 reads positions as ramps across the grid (unit ball x = column position of the lit ball cell). Layer 2 compares ball x to paddle x. The outputs pick left, stay or right, with a dead zone so the paddle doesn't jitter.
  2. Probe. v1 never drops the ball, but it catches it dead-centre, so the ball climbs almost vertically. The last 1–3 bricks of a round take most of the time.
  3. v2, constant aim. Always catch the ball off-centre. Made things worse: the ball locked into repeating loops.
  4. v3, aim at bricks. Eight column bands, each a pair of units that only "speaks" when the ball is in that band. It reports whether more bricks are to the left or right. Small gain.
  5. v4, fix the endgame. In v3 a single remaining brick barely registered (tanh(0.03)). Making one brick count fully gave the biggest jump.
  6. v5, aim harder at distant bricks. Cleared round 1 in a third of the time, but dropped more balls. Rejected.

11 practice evaluations on serves 1–6, then one held-out run on serves 101–120. The setup follows MIT's "weight-space programming" experiments (Takehi et al., Nov 2026): no gradients, no training, a small evaluation budget. The sim is copied unchanged from the arcade's src/games/Arkanoid/sim.js.
