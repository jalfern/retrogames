Teaching a lander to land

EXPERIMENTS RUN BY QWEN (the forge's RL lane, local) · DIRECTED BY JON ALFERNESS WITH CLAUDE · Oct 15–19, 2026

A PPO agent trained from scratch on the arcade's own Lunar Lander lands on a pad 94.0% of the time on 500 fresh random starts with sideways drift, against 81.0% for the game's hand-coded autopilot on the same starts. A brute-force search shows at least 97% of starts are landable, so it's within a few points of the ceiling. A network that only copied the autopilot, with no RL at all, reached 88.6% and beat its own teacher.

Getting there took seven reward and setup bugs, each found because a standard algorithm failed on a problem it should solve.

Who beats whom

Same 500 drift starts for every flyer. Policies are deterministic in eval (most likely button every frame).

The setup

  • The game. The arcade's own Lunar Lander, not Gym's. Its physics live in a pure, deterministic sim.js that steps the world from a state and a button press, with no screen involved. It runs ~3.7M steps/s in Node; training ran ~8,000 steps/s end to end.
  • What the agent sees. Eight numbers per frame: position, velocity, tilt, fuel, distance to the nearest pad, height above ground.
  • What it can do. Four buttons (nothing, rotate left, rotate right, thrust), 60 decisions a second.
  • Algorithm. PPO (Stable-Baselines3, RL Zoo's Lunar Lander settings: gamma 0.999, 16 parallel games), a two-layer 64-unit network.

A policy isn't a memorized sequence of button presses. It's a reflex: a function from "where am I right now" to "which button", asked fresh every frame. Knock it off course and it reacts to where it now is; a replay just keeps pressing the same buttons and crashes.

Bug 7 is classic reward hacking: the agent optimizes exactly what it's paid for, loopholes included. With all seven fixed, run h3 went from 0% to 75% in ~15M steps (it sat at 0% for the first 4M), then peaked at 94% at 19M.

Copy first, then RL

  • Plain copying failed; DAgger fixed it. Copying recorded autopilot flights landed 0.5%: one small error leads somewhere the teacher never flew. Having the autopilot label the student's own mistakes (DAgger) took it to 43%, 89.5%, then 91.5% in three rounds.
  • The button layout nearly sank it. The autopilot rotates and thrusts at once on 10% of frames. Squeezed into 4 one-at-a-time buttons, the autopilot itself fell from 80.5% to 6.2%. With 6 actions, copying worked.
  • Recovery examples beat volume. 1,000 clean autopilot flights: 0.0%. The same flights with 20% random actions, still labelled with the autopilot's choice: 9.4%. More clean demos (1 → 1,000 flights) only lifted RL warm-starts from 1% to 21% at 10M steps.
  • The recipe that works: copy the actor, train the critic on the copy's own flights, then PPO. A gamepad-style copy went from 87% to 94.6% after 1.25M RL steps, matching the from-scratch pilot with ~15× less RL.
  • RL can wreck a good copy. Warm-started PPO collapsed (74% → 8%) because of a fresh value network, not the copy. Keeping the best checkpoint makes the result usable; the late collapse is still open.

Does it generalize? Gravity

No retraining, same 500 starts. It stays at or near 90% across ±25% gravity, and fails in opposite ways at the extremes: at low gravity it burns at its learned cadence and flies off the top; at high gravity it under-brakes and crashes. Gravity isn't in what it sees, so it can't adapt; it only reacts to its own speed.

Does seeing the terrain help?

Not on these levels. Pilots trained on ridge terrain landed 76–81% on held-out starts whether or not they got 8 extra "ground height" inputs. Training on ridges helped; seeing them didn't, because the ridge always sits in a predictable spot before the nearest pad. Sight should only matter once terrain is unpredictable. And liftoff from scratch is luck: in four identical runs, learning started at ~45M, 91M and 105M steps, and once never. Seeding from a copy makes that start predictable.

Second game: Tempest

The recipe carried over. Qwen ported Tempest to the same training bridge in about three hours. On normal difficulty a copy of Tempest's autopilot won 100% of new games. On a harder version, copy plus RL tied the autopilot (56.3% vs 56.9% over the same 1,000 games). The copy alone stalled at 34% because it couldn't see what the teacher sees (which enemy it's chasing, which way flippers will flip); giving it that hidden information took it to 48%. A copy can only match a teacher whose information it shares.

Lessons

  1. The agent optimizes exactly what you pay for. Every failure ending must cost the same, or it will find the cheapest one.
  2. The agent discounts the future. Check reward economics the way it sees them, with gamma applied. A late penalty is a small penalty.
  3. When a standard algorithm fails on a standard-sized problem, suspect the setup first. Tuning the reward treats the symptom.
  4. Learning curves are flat, then steep. Patience beat another round of tinkering.
  5. Watch episode length: crashing is short, landing is medium, hovering runs to the cap.
